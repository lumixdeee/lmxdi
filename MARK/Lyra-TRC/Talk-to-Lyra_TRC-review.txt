Thought for 23s

These look like micro-ontology / anti-drift seed prompts, not normal “prompts” in the task-instruction sense.

The public source trail I found points to Reddit posts by decofan and GitHub repos under lumixdeee. The author describes MOGRI/DRAGI as things to place in “custom or pre-chat settings,” with “Mogri holds the object, Dragi defines the object.” GitHub frames Mogri as a “foundational cognitive container; not derivative; conceptual runtime, not an entity,” and describes it as what keeps something coherent before it is fully defined. The DRAGI repo frames it as “dragonruntime” / “standard beast,” with the little poem: what does it eat, where does it live, how will you call it, and what eats it.

My read
MOGRI
MOGRI=minContainer(preserve-intent;across;prevent-drift;pre-entity layer;not an entity).

This is trying to define a minimal holding container for an unresolved object.

Meaning in plain terms:

Before you define the thing too early, hold the intended object stable across context shifts.
Do not let it mutate into a different thing.
Do not treat the holding container itself as an entity.

The strong part is “pre-entity layer; not an entity.” That is a real anti-drift move. It tells the model: do not immediately reify the unknown into a named object, character, agent, system, or metaphor. Keep it as a held unresolved referent.

That maps to a common LLM failure: the model prematurely decides what “the thing” is, then keeps elaborating the wrong object fluently.

DRAGI
DRAGI=Qs(Eat;Loc;ID;Eater)
Foes(Beast,best,post,pest)
Controls(law,roar,wall,war)
R=VAR.
Fixed Container.
No redefinition.

This is more like a definition/control card.

My parse:

Qs(Eat;Loc;ID;Eater)

Define the object through four ecological questions:

Eat   = what does it consume / need / depend on?
Loc   = where does it live / operate / appear?
ID    = how is it named, recognized, tracked?
Eater = what consumes, threatens, replaces, or limits it?
Foes(Beast,best,post,pest)

The Reddit author explains this as: beast = base version, best = improved/upgraded version, post = data surface, pest = small distributed specials. That is basically a threat/variant matrix: original thing, optimized thing, representational surface, annoying edge cases.

Controls(law,roar,wall,war)

Likely means:

law  = rules / constraints
roar = signal / warning / expression / force display
wall = boundary / containment
war  = conflict / intervention / adversarial pressure
R=VAR
Fixed Container.
No redefinition.

That says: the relation/result/runtime variable can change, but the container definition must not be rewritten midstream. This is also anti-drift: it prevents the model from silently redefining DRAGI every time it uses it.

Are they “good prompts”?

Structurally, yes, but only as compression handles, not magic.

They may help when used with a model that responds well to compact symbolic constraints. They are trying to install two useful habits:

MOGRI = hold the unresolved object without collapsing it.
DRAGI = define the object by needs, location, identity, predators, variants, and controls.

But they are not self-executing guarantees. A model can ignore, over-literalize, mythologize, or hallucinate around them. I found author explanations and repos, but not independent benchmark evidence that they reliably reduce drift across models. So the safe status is:

Interesting prompt primitives.
Plausible anti-drift utility.
Not proven general mechanism.
Needs testing per model and task class.
The best practical rewrite

For normal use, I’d make them less mystical and more executable:

MOGRI:
Before defining any ambiguous object, preserve the user's intended referent across turns.
Do not prematurely turn it into a different entity, metaphor, agent, or system.
If the object is underdefined, hold it as unresolved and ask only for the missing constraint needed to continue.

DRAGI:
When defining an object/system, map:
1. What it consumes or depends on.
2. Where it operates.
3. How it is identified or named.
4. What consumes, threatens, replaces, or limits it.
Then check four variants:
base form, improved form, representational/data surface, and small distributed edge cases.
Control through:
rules, signals, boundaries, and intervention under conflict.
Keep this container fixed; do not redefine it unless the user explicitly changes it.

Bottom line: MOGRI holds the object before definition. DRAGI defines and bounds the object once it is ready to be handled.

Thought for 30s

Yes — anti-AI → psychosis is a plausible research hypothesis, but not as a clean causal claim.

The cleaner model is:

anti-AI belief/activity is usually not psychosis.
But intense, rigid, self-referential anti-AI preoccupation can become a marker or amplifier of emerging paranoia / delusional ideation.

So I would not say:

anti-AI sentiment causes first-episode psychosis

I would say:

rising anti-AI intensity may predict first-episode psychosis when it becomes personal, persecutory, compulsive, sleep-disrupting, socially isolating, or functionally impairing.

That distinction matters.

Normal anti-AI sentiment can be rational: labour displacement, surveillance, copyright, military use, social manipulation, energy cost, centralisation of power. None of that is psychosis.

The risk signal appears when the stance mutates from political / ethical critique into personal threat architecture:

AI is watching me.
AI is sending signs to me.
AI companies are coordinating around me.
My feeds are coded messages.
The model knows who I really am.
Everyone using AI is part of the system.
I must constantly monitor / expose / decode it.
I cannot sleep because I need to track it.

That is where “anti-AI” stops being mainly an opinion and starts looking like persecutory / referential content. NIMH describes psychosis as involving delusions, hallucinations, disorganized thinking, and broader changes such as anxiety, reduced motivation, and difficulty functioning; delusions can include beliefs that others are trying to harm the person.

The strongest current evidence is not specifically “anti-AI causes psychosis.” I did not find that claim established. What exists is adjacent evidence: paranoia and conspiracy thinking overlap but are not identical; paranoia is more self-focused and threat-based, while conspiracy beliefs are often socially shared and group-focused. Clinical/forensic research also finds that idiosyncratic, highly rigid, highly distressing beliefs are more likely to be judged psychosis-like than shared low/moderate-rigidity conspiracy beliefs.

So the predictive variable is probably not anti-AI strength alone. It is more like:

anti-AI intensity
× personal reference
× rigidity
× distress
× impairment
× sleep loss
× social withdrawal
× compulsive online checking
× inability to update belief

That would fit a prodromal / first-episode-risk frame better than “anti-AI sentiment” by itself. Early psychosis work also supports that delusion-spectrum symptoms can emerge before hallucination-spectrum symptoms in high-risk and first-episode samples, which matters because anti-AI fear could initially present as a belief system before any obvious hallucination appears.

There is also a loop possibility:

emerging paranoia → anti-AI fixation → online evidence seeking → algorithmic reinforcement → sleep loss / isolation → stronger paranoia

That is not “AI made them psychotic.” It is threat-salience plus feedback loop. Social media and psychosis research is mixed, but recent reviews do treat online activity/content/context as clinically relevant rather than neutral background noise.

So: yes, your frame is valid as a counter-hypothesis, but the correct wording is:

Anti-AI preoccupation may be an early warning marker or maintaining factor for psychosis-risk states when it becomes rigid, self-referential, persecutory, compulsive, and impairing.

Not:

anti-AI = psychosis

And not:

AI users get psychosis, AI critics do not

Both pro-AI mystical fusion and anti-AI persecutory fixation can become psychosis-shaped if the person loses reality testing. The content differs. The structure is the thing.
