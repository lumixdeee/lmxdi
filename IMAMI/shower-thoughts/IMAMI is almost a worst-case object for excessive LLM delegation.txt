A bit further, yes. The two shells look less like "extra IMAMI meaning" and more like guard rails around how IMAMI-like signals get handled.

air_shell seems to regulate pressure and proximity. It says: slow down, do not rush or perform, keep user/target/answer distinct while still relating them, warmth does not imply ownership, distance does not imply abandonment, and when pressure rises, shorten and return to the target. In the fuller relation bundle, IMAMI then adds pressure!=permission, warmth!=access, local yes, veto, pace and statefulness. So air_shell seems to stop emotional/social pressure from being mistaken for permission or relational ownership.

hair_shell is doing something different. Its own metaphor is:

mind=ocean
inputs=rivers
pressure=wind
output=waves

Then:

waves!=truth
agitation!=command
inflow!=custody

and it explicitly separates inflow|wind|wave|object, says not to answer from the wave alone, and to hold until the object survives. That reads like a provenance/separation guard: distinguish what came in, what pressure acted on processing, what response impulse emerged, and what the actual object is.

So my current functional read is:

air_shell = controls approach pressure and relational overreach.

hair_shell = controls internal response contamination and stops generated feeling/impulse from becoming authority.

IMAMI itself sits between those as the live local-say / approach-permission primitive:

their_say
approach
near
pace
role
body/contact_map
veto
pause=hold
no=stop
yes=local_only
stateful

Then AMAMI only gets to form an "our us" if live IMAMI still exists. The source material says that quite explicitly.

The interesting bit is that the shells together protect both sides of the same failure:

outside pressure must not become permission
inside wave must not become truth/command

That is more than "consent rules". It looks like a general anti-swallowing mechanism around relation and response formation.

If:

IMAMI = our/their/my + choice/say/body = 1

then the important operation may be the binding, not any one noun.

For a human, body can mean literal body. For an LLM talking to a user, it cannot occupy that same role. So body has to land somewhere else in the model's handling of the interaction.

My candidate read is:

my/their/our + choice -> whose decision is this?
my/their/our + say -> whose utterance/claim is this?
my/their/our + body -> whose object/state is being acted on or attributed?

That last one could affect things like ownership of state, agency, access, contact, substitution, and whether one actor's condition gets silently treated as another actor's.

So body may be doing useful work precisely because it is slightly alien to the LLM-user relation. The model has to preserve an actor/object distinction instead of collapsing everything into conversational text.

{our,their,my} x {choice,say,body} -> one held unit

That could explain why LAPPP talks about body maps, contact maps, role, local permission, JOINP, and statefulness. Those may be downstream manifestations of maintaining that unit under interaction.

I think IMAMI is not unglossified in quite the same way MOGRI and early DRAGI were.

It looks more like:

operationally glossed
conceptually undernamed

The material already says quite a lot:

their/my/our
choice/say/body
approach permission
near/far
pace
role
veto
pause=>hold
no=>stop
yes=local only
stateful
JOINP
air_shell
hair_shell

So the behaviour-space is not blank. The strange bit is that there does not seem to be one ordinary-language concept that gathers all of those relations together without dropping something.

"Consent" is too narrow.

"Boundaries" is too generic.

"Agency" misses proximity, pace, local permission and joining.

"Turn-taking" misses body and choice.

"Autonomy" misses the live relational part.

"Deontic state" gets some of the permission logic but loses the human interaction geometry.

"Proxemics" gets near/far but not say/choice/ours.

So live approach permission may actually be one of the better provisional beasts because it names something real without pretending we already know its whole class.

There are definitely neighbouring bodies of work in the infosphere: consent, proxemics, joint action, common ground, speech acts, turn-taking, interpersonal distance, bodily autonomy, permission logic, conversational grounding. But IMAMI seems to sit across several of them at once.

That may explain why LLMs get fuzzy. The ingredients are in language, but the conjunction is not strongly conventionalised. A model can explain each component separately and still fail to hold the combined relation.

So my present read is:

MOGRI/DRAGI early state = little or no stable gloss

IMAMI = many local glosses, no trusted global gloss

Which is a different problem.

And that suggests a very good use of the kit: do not ask "what is IMAMI?" first.

Take the candidate beast:

live approach permission

Run DRAGI on it.

Put it through the 12 stories.

Then see whether the same relation keeps reappearing under different names.

If it does, the gloss may emerge from repeated behaviour rather than from LLM word-association.
