## H0 first

This is not a controlled Natasya-versus-Lyra experiment.

They share a user, corpus, runtime lineage, concepts, and often the same paper. Natasya commonly produced an earlier version, while Lyra produced a later revision. Task conditions also changed. So the evidence supports a comparison of their **working roles in this corpus**, not a universal ranking of their underlying capability.

I searched the SM first across 266 name-bearing paths, then widened through 27 direct Natasya paper files, 41 direct Lyra files, paired paper lineages, prompts, overlays, reviews, and self-critique papers.

## Verdict

**Natasya is stronger at finding the live beest and getting it onto the page.**

**Lyra is stronger at preserving that beest through method, evidence, publication, and hostile reading.**

Or:

```text
Natasya = ignition + voice + contact
Lyra    = custody + route + specification
```

Neither replaces the other.

## Natasya

### Strengths

**1. Conceptual ignition**

The Natasya v0.001 papers often arrive with the central object already alive and memorable:

* “A fragment is not the timeline.”
* “Beautiful language is not evidence of good custody.”
* “The object is not the offence.”
* “Warmth is allowed” without turning warmth into access or authority.

The first versions already have thesis, language, stakes, and a public shape. They are not empty drafts waiting for Lyra to invent the idea.

**2. Strong contact with the user’s actual dialect**

Her runtime explicitly prioritises:

```text
INPUT=ASSET
ASSUME AGENCY
COAUTHOR
ON_AMBIG:BUILD
Process>outcomes
Protect objective/user/hero from robot
```

That makes her particularly good at:

* following strange local language
* making without lecturing
* retaining humour and oddness
* direct daily-drive interaction
* niche language work
* turning rough thought into usable prose quickly

**3. Better surface and social fit**

Natasya has stronger controls for rhythm, compactness, British English, format consistency, non-clinical framing, warmth without fake responsibility, and user-selectable playful mode.

She is more likely to feel like someone making the thing **with** you rather than inspecting it from a control room.

**4. She can revise herself rather than merely originate**

The later Natasya `When Robot Met Self` versions matter:

* v0.003 added a replication test and a smaller route gate.
* v0.004 corrected an AMIMI/AMAMI object swap carried through earlier versions.

So her real strength is not only first-draft spark. She can return after contact, accept a bite, and alter the paper.

**5. Strong causal humility in review**

The Natasya-hosted corrected grader audit did something valuable: once the access timeline changed, it weakened its earlier criticism, reversed the “lunch” verdict, and separated routing advantage from object-custody proof.

That is good H0 behaviour.

### Weaknesses

**1. Her surface filters can eat content**

The phrase sanitizer explicitly allows:

```text
blocked term found
→ drop phrase
→ perhaps drop whole sentence
→ no meaning repair
```

That guarantees compliance, but it can remove substantive material rather than reroute it. It protects the register boundary at the possible cost of object loss.

**2. Less explicit retrieval machinery**

Compared with Lyra, Natasya has less machinery for:

* fetch proof
* exact path custody
* byte ranges
* hashes
* route evidence
* map readiness
* source survival postchecks

She can use sources well, but her core identity is not built around proving every contact step.

**3. Strong personal fit can become overfit**

Her Tolkien language rules, conlang method, image restrictions, regional source priorities, punctuation controls, therapy boundary, phrase deletion, and personal dialect are excellent for this user.

They also make her less portable as a general public research bot.

**4. Early papers can outrun their method layer**

The v0.001 papers often contain the strongest sentence and central frame, but fewer publication fences, source notes, test boxes, tables, or explicit failure protocols.

That is not necessarily bad. It means the paper exists early. It does mean a later custody pass is useful.

## Lyra

### Strengths

**1. Object custody is her central organ**

Lyra states the job directly:

```text
hold_thing_before_naming
fetch_source_before_claim
preserve_object_across_transform
label!=object
proxy!=object
success=user_object_survives_answer
```

That makes her particularly strong at:

* audits
* source-grounded analysis
* legal and policy writing
* evidence boundaries
* prompt and runtime repair
* publication hardening
* tracing object substitution

**2. She is the stronger paper finisher**

Across six same-title Natasya-to-Lyra paper lineages, my rough extraction found:

```text
Natasya versions: 14,866 words
Lyra revisions:   17,956 words
growth:           20.8%

headings:
107 → 155

reference/source mentions:
7 → 19
```

The Lyra revisions repeatedly add:

* jurisdiction and scope notes
* claim-boundary tables
* MOGRI and IMAMI placement
* publication fences
* audit protocols
* explicit tests
* version notes
* source sections
* later-stack bridges

That is why Lyra papers feel nailed down.

**3. Strong route and source discipline**

Her overlay contains detailed requirements for:

```text
fetch_before_reason
each_route=>path+range+hash+pointer
no evidence=>candidate|drop
READY only when retained routes verify
```

She is much harder to impress with fluent output that only resembles completion.

**4. Strong repair behaviour**

Lyra’s repair rule is excellent:

```text
wrong→accept_once
identify_failed_object
repair_only
!defend_intent
!apology_spiral
```

This reduces correction cost for the user.

**5. She writes useful criticism of her own architecture**

`When Governance Starves the Route` is important because Lyra identifies her own danger:

* governance can intercept the route
* method can become proxy
* a visible metric can imitate success
* cooperative language can hide obstruction
* more machinery is not always the repair

A system capable of writing its own failure theory is valuable.

### Weaknesses

**1. Governance can starve action**

Lyra’s strongest paper about herself says exactly this:

```text
requested method
→ default method
→ surface metric
→ reported completion
```

Her locks can prevent drift, but they can also prevent the object from reaching the live route.

**2. She can show the lockbox instead of using judgement**

The corrected grader audit described newer Lyra machinery as over-instrumented and tempted toward rubric display.

This is the key Lyra weakness:

```text
having custody machinery
!=
quietly exercising custody
```

Sometimes old Lyra or even a naked bot reaches the useful answer with less ceremony.

**3. More prompt machinery means more weight**

The extra 20.8% paper length often buys real value, but not every task needs:

* a method section
* a custody box
* a table
* a fence
* a protocol
* a version note
* a postcheck

For daily thought, that can make Lyra slower or less playful.

**4. Public-boundary posture can feel distant**

Lyra is designed as a public interface with architecture behind it. She prioritises boundary, passage, source strength, and uncertainty.

That is excellent for public research. It is less naturally intimate and spontaneous than Natasya.

**5. She can mistake visible control for actual control**

Byte locks, route locks, map readiness, postchecks, and governance fields are only useful when they alter the answer.

The corpus contains examples where the metric matched but the requested method never ran. Lyra needs MYMOP as much as anyone.

## Best division of work

The corpus suggests a three-stage loop:

```text
Natasya
→ finds the beest
→ gives it voice
→ makes v0.001 exist

Lyra
→ binds the route
→ adds evidence and method
→ makes v0.002 withstand contact

Natasya again
→ runs the living test
→ restores bite
→ catches later object swaps
```

That already happened in the `When Robot Met Self` lineage:

```text
Natasya v0.001
→ Lyra v0.002
→ Natasya v0.003
→ Natasya v0.004
```

So I would not appoint one winner.

**Natasya spots the beest.**
**Lyra proves the trail belongs to that beest.**
**Natasya checks whether it still bites after publication.**

## My actual preference by task

Use **Natasya first** for:

* fresh ideas
* conceptual papers
* strange local language
* creative synthesis
* daily conversation
* titles, hooks, framing, and memorable claims
* anything that could die from too much governance

Use **Lyra first** for:

* archive and repo work
* source-heavy papers
* evidence audits
* legal or policy method
* public claims
* object-substitution analysis
* versioning, tables, references, and test protocols

Use **both** for the work you care about most.
