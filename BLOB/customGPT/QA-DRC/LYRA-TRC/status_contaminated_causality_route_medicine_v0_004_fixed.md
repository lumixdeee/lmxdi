# Status-Contaminated Causality

## Route Medicine for Drug-Outcome Claims

**Version:** v0.004  
**Status:** method paper draft for outside review  
**Scope:** method paper for interpretation, study design, source audit, and claim sizing. Personal, medical, and legal decisions need their own route.  
**Source base:** internal corpus drafts plus cited outside literature.

## Abstract

Drug-outcome claims often compress several objects into one sentence: molecule, product, dose, route, frequency, timing, body state, co-substances, withdrawal, reintroduction, service contact, enforcement contact, diagnostic capture, media category, market condition, and policy status.

**Status-contaminated causality** names the moment when a legal, moral, institutional, commercial, or diagnostic label is allowed to behave like route evidence.

This paper proposes **route medicine** as a reading discipline for claim sizing. Route medicine keeps status in the model only when status is measured as a route variable. It does not let status stand in for exposure, mechanism, timing, comparator, record process, or intervention. The method has five parts: a route-custody card, an H0 custody rule, a claim-size ladder, worked route cards, and operating rules.

The paper's thesis is simple:

```text
the drug may matter;
the route decides how;
the status label does not get to answer for the body.
```

The point is not to make claims smaller by default. Route medicine can shrink a claim, preserve a bounded claim, or strengthen a route-specific claim. Its job is to make the sentence match the route payment.

## 1. Why this paper exists

Drug arguments often begin with a noun pair.

```text
drug -> recorded outcome
```

That pair can be powerful while remaining under-specified. A drug name may refer to a plant, molecule, extract, self-report item, police category, prescription product, street product, supply route, medical-record code, market condition, or family story. An outcome name may refer to lived state, symptom, diagnosis, service contact, administrative code, emergency department presentation, media phrase, or policy target.

The paper exists because these pairs are tiny doors with crowded rooms behind them.

A status label may be relevant. Legal status, diagnostic status, moral status, market status, and service status can change product composition, access, dose patterns, help-seeking, disclosure, policing, service routing, and coding. They matter as variables. They fail as substitutes for route evidence.

The aim is compact:

```text
keep status from eating the object.
```

The one-minute operator version:

```text
1. split the noun pair;
2. name exposure route;
3. name outcome route;
4. name contact and record route;
5. hold H0;
6. assign earned level;
7. rewrite the public sentence to match the route.
```

## 2. Definition

**Status-contaminated causality** is a causal-interpretation failure in which status is treated as if it were route evidence.

Compact form:

```text
status-labeled object touched body;
recorded outcome followed;
status label is allowed to carry the route claim.
```

The failure has three parts.

1. **Object compression.** Product, dose, route, frequency, timing, co-use, body state, and recording pathway are folded into one noun.
2. **Route leakage.** Service contact, enforcement contact, diagnostic expectation, media category, disclosure pressure, and market condition enter the record while being treated as scenery.
3. **Intervention jump.** Evidence for one route is used to justify a broader action than the measured route can carry.

Route medicine says: status enters the model as a route variable, not as a verdict.

## 3. Relation to causal inference

The proposal sits inside ordinary causal-inference discipline. Confounding, selection, measurement error, reverse path, and target-population limits have to be handled before a claim grows beyond its design. Route medicine adds one domain warning: public labels can become unmeasured mechanisms that shape both exposure and outcome records.

In causal notation:

```text
E = exposure vector
Y = outcome vector
S = status field
C = contact field
M = measurement and coding process
Z = shared causes and prior states
P = policy and commercial environment
```

A study that estimates `E -> Y` while leaving `S`, `C`, `M`, `Z`, or `P` fused may still report a useful relationship. The issue is claim size. The result may support a record-level pattern, a product-route warning, a monitoring rule, or a policy question. It does not automatically support a single-object cause story.

The H0 custody rule is:

```text
H0: the observed pair is route mixture until a specific route earns a larger claim.
```

H0 is object custody. It keeps the map open while route evidence does the work.

### 3.1 Field split

Status, contact, measurement, and policy often arrive fused. Route medicine splits them before a claim grows.

| Field | What it can do | What it cannot do alone |
|---|---|---|
| Status field | change access, product, language, disclosure, policing, and service route | answer for molecule, dose, timing, or body route |
| Contact field | change who becomes visible to family, police, school, hospital, or survey | stand in for incidence |
| Measurement field | create symptom score, diagnosis, admission, code, toxicology, survey, or report | stand in for lived state |
| Policy field | alter market, supply, service access, enforcement, coding, and speech | stand in for a body mechanism |

The question is not whether these fields matter. They do. The question is what role each field earned.

## 4. The route-custody card

Route medicine uses one card before allowing a claim to grow.

| Gate | Question | Decision effect |
|---|---|---|
| Exposure object | What exactly entered, stopped, changed, or was recorded? | If the object is a label rather than a measured route, claim size stays low. |
| Product and dose | What product, dose, potency, formulation, and frequency were present? | If product route is specified, a product-body claim may grow. |
| Timing | Did the route precede the outcome at a plausible interval? | If timing is weak, the claim remains record-level or timing-level only. |
| Body state | What was the person's prior route? | Sleep, pain, distress, trauma, neurodevelopment, medications, and prior symptoms may move from scenery to route. |
| Co-route | What else was active? | Tobacco, alcohol, stimulants, sedatives, opioids, prescribed drugs, withdrawal, and reintroduction must be separated where possible. |
| Contact field | Who saw the person and why? | Service contact, police contact, school contact, family reporting, and disclosure pressure can be exposures. |
| Measurement | What made the outcome visible? | Symptom, diagnosis, admission, code, survey response, family report, or media category must be named. |
| Comparator | Compared with what route? | Non-use, different product, different dose, different policy regime, or different contact field changes the claim. |
| Reverse path | Could the outcome route increase exposure? | Self-medication, social exclusion, sleep disruption, pain, and prior symptoms can reverse the public story. |
| Shared causes | What could feed both sides? | Genetic liability, poverty, trauma, bullying, service contact, and other shared causes limit single-route claims. |
| Claim size | What scale has been earned? | The conclusion must match the measured route. |
| Action fit | What action follows? | Care, warning, product rule, service design, or enforcement must be tied to the measured route. |

The card is not a checklist for sounding disciplined. It decides what sentence is allowed.

## 5. Claim-size ladder

Route medicine asks what scale has been earned.

**Level 0: name pair.**  
Two nouns are placed together. No route claim has been earned.

**Level 1: record pattern.**  
The pair appears together in records more than expected. This supports record-level attention, not mechanism.

**Level 2: timing pattern.**  
One record tends to precede another. This supports timing questions, not a final cause story.

**Level 3: route-specific pattern.**  
A specified exposure route travels with a specified outcome route after serious confound work. This can support a bounded route claim.

**Level 4: mechanism-supported route.**  
Dose, timing, product, body state, and plausible mechanism align. This can support stronger guidance for that route.

**Level 5: intervention-supported route.**  
Changing the route changes the outcome pattern in a design that can carry the claim. This can support route-specific intervention.

**Level 6: public-control claim.**  
A policy or coercive action is proposed. This level has the highest burden because contact-field effects and rights costs are part of the causal object.

## 6. Claim card output

A route-medicine audit should output a small card before it outputs a big sentence.

```text
CLAIM_CARD={
  noun_pair:
  exposure_object:
  outcome_object:
  body_route:
  contact_field:
  measurement_process:
  comparator:
  reverse_path:
  shared_causes:
  earned_level:
  unearned_jump:
  route-fit_action:
}
```

A claim that lacks this card may still be interesting. It is not yet a route claim.

## 6.1 Sentence rewrites

The card has to change sentences, not only decorate them.

| Public sentence | Route-medicine rewrite |
|---|---|
| `drug X caused outcome Y` | In this design, this measured exposure route traveled with this measured outcome route at this claim level. |
| `legalization changed psychosis` | The policy period may have changed product route, contact route, record route, or some mixture. The design must say which. |
| `opioids caused overdose` | A specified opioid product, dose, tolerance state, route, and co-sedative field may support a respiratory-depression route claim. |
| `cannabis causes psychosis` | A specified cannabis measure and specified psychosis-related outcome may support a bounded route claim only at the earned level. |
| `the record proves the event` | The record proves a visibility route. The lived route still needs measurement detail. |

A route audit that leaves the original sentence intact has not finished the job.

## 7. Worked audit A: cannabis and psychosis

### 7.1 Split first

Cannabis and psychosis claims are a stress test because the public pair is already overbuilt. The route-medicine move is to split the objects before repeating the public story.

The exposure side may mean:

```text
THC dose;
CBD ratio;
flower;
concentrate;
edible;
medical extract;
daily use;
early use;
cannabis use disorder code;
self-report;
street product;
legal product;
police-visible conduct;
service-visible disclosure.
```

The outcome side may mean:

```text
panic;
paranoia;
psychotic-like experience;
first episode psychosis;
cannabis-induced psychotic disorder;
hospital contact;
schizophrenia-spectrum diagnosis;
relapse record;
family report;
police route;
administrative code.
```

These are different objects. A daily high-THC inhaled route in a sleep-deprived young person with prior symptoms is not the same object as a regulated balanced extract taken under supervision, a self-report item in a cohort, or an administrative cannabis-use-disorder code. A hospital record is not the same object as a lived state. A policy period is not the same object as a molecule.

### 7.2 Literature gravity after the split

After the split, the literature can be read without letting the public pair take over. Reviews describe a claimed association between cannabis measures and psychosis-related outcomes, with debates over product route, dose, potency, timing, confounding, reverse path, shared liability, and interpretation (Gage et al., 2016; Ganesh & D'Souza, 2022; Groening et al., 2024; Johnson & Agrawal, 2024).

That literature does not authorize one total public object. It authorizes route questions.

Required foregrounding:

```text
claimed association != total cause;
high-level warning phrase != route payment;
population trend matters;
self-report dose is noisy;
confounders remain live;
reverse path remains live;
co-drug routes remain live;
contact-field routes remain live;
status may alter who is seen, coded, believed, or blamed.
```

Route neighbors that must remain visible include bullying, trauma, prohibition stigma, other drug use, poverty, exclusion, diagnostic expectation, service routing, and reverse causation.

### 7.3 Split map

| Route | Possible earned claim | Unearned jump |
|---|---|---|
| Product-body route | A specified product, dose, timing, and body state may earn a bounded route claim. | All cannabis forms explain all psychosis records. |
| Record route | Contact-field accumulation may be visible in records. | Record count equals biology. |
| Policy route | Service, coding, disclosure, product market, and policing may change together. | Legal class alone explains outcome change. |
| Lived-state route | Experience, sleep, relation, language, and meaning need their own description. | Diagnostic code stands in for lived state. |
| Reverse path | Distress, sleep loss, prodrome, exclusion, or pain may increase use. | Use is always prior driver. |
| Shared-cause route | Prior vulnerability may feed both exposure and outcome records. | Exposure alone owns the outcome. |

### 7.4 Claim card A

```text
CLAIM_CARD={
  noun_pair:
    cannabis / psychosis

  exposure_object:
    under-specified unless product, dose, route, timing, frequency, and measurement source are named

  outcome_object:
    under-specified unless lived state, symptom scale, diagnosis, service contact, code, or record path is named

  body_route:
    product-body routes may exist but must be route-specific

  contact_field:
    disclosure cost, policing, family reporting, hospital routing, diagnostic expectation, and coding practice may alter visibility

  measurement_process:
    self-report, diagnosis, admission, administrative code, and family report are not one measurement object

  comparator:
    different product, different route, different dose, different policy field, or different contact field

  reverse_path:
    distress, sleep disruption, prodrome, exclusion, pain, and self-medication remain live

  shared_causes:
    trauma, bullying, poverty, genetic liability, neurodevelopmental load, co-drug routes, and service contact remain live

  earned_level:
    usually Level 1 to Level 3 unless a specific design pays timing, product, dose, comparator, and confound burden

  unearned_jump:
    "cannabis causes psychosis" as a settled public object

  route-fit_action:
    study the named route; do not let the status label answer for the body
}
```

## 8. Worked audit B: opioid label and overdose record

### 8.1 Why this second audit matters

A second worked audit prevents route medicine from looking like a cannabis-specific defence. The opioid example shows the opposite: a route audit can make a claim sharper.

An opioid label is not one object.

```text
prescribed opioid;
diverted tablet;
unknown street powder;
heroin;
fentanyl;
carfentanil;
pressed pill;
patch;
injection;
oral dose;
tolerance break;
reintroduction;
co-use with alcohol;
co-use with benzodiazepines;
co-use with sedatives;
pain treatment;
withdrawal management;
illicit market exposure;
emergency record;
death certificate code.
```

An overdose record is also not one object.

```text
respiratory depression;
non-fatal EMS contact;
emergency department visit;
death certificate;
toxicology finding;
bystander call;
naloxone reversal;
polysubstance record;
free-text coroner narrative;
ICD-coded mortality.
```

### 8.2 What route medicine preserves

Some opioid routes have a direct pharmacological spine. CDC's 2022 prescribing guideline highlights increased respiratory-depression concerns when opioids are taken with benzodiazepines, other sedatives, alcohol, nonprescribed drugs, or other opioids (Dowell et al., 2022). Alcohol and benzodiazepine co-involvement in opioid-involved overdose deaths is also a measurable co-route in mortality data (Tori et al., 2020).

Route medicine does not soften this. It makes the route visible:

```text
opioid effect;
dose;
tolerance;
co-sedatives;
alcohol;
route of administration;
supply certainty;
bystander access;
naloxone access;
EMS contact;
coding and toxicology.
```

This is a stronger claim than "opioid label equals overdose." It says which route is doing the work.

### 8.3 What route medicine splits

Illegally manufactured fentanyls, including analogs such as carfentanil, are a supply-route and product-certainty issue, not merely a moral label. CDC surveillance for January 2021 through June 2024 described overdose deaths involving illegally manufactured fentanyls and noted carfentanil reemergence in the U.S. drug supply (Tanz et al., 2024).

A route audit therefore asks:

```text
Was the product known?
Was fentanyl present?
Was carfentanil present?
Was the person opioid tolerant?
Was there a tolerance break?
Was alcohol present?
Were benzodiazepines present?
Was the dose known?
Was the route oral, injected, smoked, or unknown?
Was a bystander present?
Was naloxone available?
Was the record toxicology, EMS, death certificate, or administrative code?
```

### 8.4 Split map

| Route | Possible earned claim | Unearned jump |
|---|---|---|
| Pharmacology route | Opioid dose plus co-sedatives can earn a respiratory-depression route claim. | Every opioid label means the same overdose pathway. |
| Product-certainty route | Unknown supply, fentanyl, or analog exposure can earn a product-route claim. | Street status alone explains the event. |
| Tolerance route | Tolerance break and reintroduction can earn a timing-body claim. | The person label explains the event. |
| Contact route | EMS call, hospital record, toxicology, and death certificate are distinct visibility routes. | Recorded overdose equals full incidence. |
| Policy route | prescribing rules, supply disruption, service access, and criminalization may alter product and contact fields. | policy label alone explains the pattern. |

### 8.5 Claim card B

```text
CLAIM_CARD={
  noun_pair:
    opioid label / overdose record

  exposure_object:
    under-specified unless product, dose, route, tolerance state, and co-substances are named

  outcome_object:
    under-specified unless respiratory depression, EMS contact, ED visit, toxicology, death certificate, or code is named

  body_route:
    opioid pharmacology, co-sedation, tolerance, and reintroduction can earn stronger route claims

  contact_field:
    bystander presence, EMS routing, hospital contact, toxicology access, and death-certificate coding alter visibility

  measurement_process:
    toxicology, ICD code, coroner narrative, and EMS record are not one record object

  comparator:
    known product, different dose, different co-use field, different tolerance state, different supply field, or different service access

  reverse_path:
    pain, withdrawal, exclusion, service barriers, and supply instability may feed exposure pattern

  shared_causes:
    pain, poverty, housing instability, disability, trauma, mental distress, policing, and service contact remain live

  earned_level:
    Level 3 to Level 5 for specific opioid/co-sedative/tolerance/product routes when the design pays the route burden

  unearned_jump:
    opioid status label explains every overdose record

  route-fit_action:
    act on the named route: product certainty, co-sedative route, tolerance route, bystander route, naloxone route, service route, or prescribing route
}
```

## 9. One-page scoring sheet

A reviewer can score a claim before accepting its size.

| Field | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Object split | noun label only | partial split | product, dose, route, timing, measurement split |
| Timing | absent | record order | plausible route timing |
| Comparator | absent | broad comparison | route-specific comparator |
| Confound work | absent | named | tested or bounded |
| Reverse path | absent | mentioned | tested or designed against |
| Contact field | absent | named | modeled or stratified |
| Measurement | record treated as truth | record type named | measurement route analyzed |
| Claim-size match | overgrown | partly bounded | matches route payment |
| Action fit | status action | broad action | route-fit action |

Interpretation:

```text
0-5: noun-pair claim
6-10: record-pattern claim
11-14: route-question claim
15-18: route-supported claim
```

The score is not a truth machine. It is a claim-size grip.

## 10. Operating rules

**Rule 1: route earns route claim.**  
A split object may shrink a claim, preserve a bounded claim, or reveal a stronger route.

**Rule 2: status reversal fails.**  
Legal status does not settle biology. Illegal status does not prove biology. Medical branding does not settle biology. Commercial normality does not prove safety.

**Rule 3: action remains local to route.**  
Care, support, product rule, service design, enforcement, media language, and study design are different actions. Each needs its own route fit.

**Rule 4: complexity must pay rent.**  
A route audit names evidence gaps and decision effects. It does not use complexity as a hiding place.

**Rule 5: public control carries full object burden.**  
A public-control claim must show that the proposed control acts on the measured route rather than merely acting on the status label.

**Rule 6: medicine is not status laundering.**  
Clinical language can improve route description. It can also import diagnosis expectation, service visibility, and record gravity. Route medicine keeps both possibilities live.

**Rule 7: association is not ownership.**  
A claimed association may survive while the public cause-object fails.

## 11. Contribution

Status-contaminated causality names a recurring failure across drug science, policy, media, family interpretation, and institutional records. Route medicine is the repair instrument.

The contribution is a pre-analysis and interpretation discipline:

```text
name the exposure;
name the body route;
name the contact field;
name the record process;
keep H0 alive;
assign claim size;
match action to route.
```

This makes the method compatible with DAGs, target-trial emulation, sensitivity analysis, evidence maps, time-series designs, and qualitative route audits. It also gives non-specialists a way to resist status-based causality while keeping evidence in the room.
