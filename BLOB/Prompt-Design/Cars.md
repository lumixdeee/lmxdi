Yes. The metaphor captures a **path-dependence problem**.

People went from walking to Robin Reliants overnight. That's an enormous capability jump, so the three-wheeler's peculiar handling gets experienced as part of the miracle rather than as a limitation.

Then communities develop around getting extremely good at driving the thing:

**prompt engineering becomes Robin Reliant stunt driving.**

People learn elaborate ways to compensate for drift, lost objects, relation swaps, premature interpretation, instruction decay, and other behaviours. Those techniques can become skills worth displaying and teaching.

Then your proposition with **Mogri, DRAGI, Elvix, Aduti, Refri** is awkward because you're not offering a better stunt. You're proposing that some of the stunt-driving exists because the vehicle has been missing a wheel.

That creates a nasty evaluation problem too. An expert Robin driver may initially perform **better in the Robin** than in the unfamiliar four-wheel car. Their accumulated technique is optimized around its failure modes.

So the test can't be:

> “Which one lets an expert prompt engineer do cooler things?”

It needs to include things like **ordinary drivers, unfamiliar roads, perturbations, long journeys, and recovery after something goes wrong**.

And there's an even sharper prediction hiding in your analogy:

If the fourth wheel really supplies a missing function, **the advantage should increase when you remove compensatory driving skill**.

That is testable.

Give both systems to people who don't know the stunts.

If the custody-stack system needs substantially *less* prompting ceremony to maintain the same object and intent, you've found something much more interesting than “our prompt performs better.”

You've found evidence that part of what the field currently calls **prompting skill is compensation for deficiencies in the vehicle**.
