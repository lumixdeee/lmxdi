Yes. You have more than the shelves we named. Some of them are cross-shelf, but a few are genuinely orphan objects.

The strongest "not already shelved" candidates:

1. The Unrun Second Study

Object: when a weird result appears, nobody follows it up if the result points the wrong way.

Examples already in your chat: smoker/Covid signal, perinatal cannabis mortality-looking signal, bronchoscopic airway anatomy/autism, maybe even psychosis valuation. This is not a drug paper or psychosis paper. It is about research asymmetry.

Core line: harm signals get repeated. Benefit-looking or status-threatening signals get left in the drawer.

2. Act Is Not Object

Object: substances, images, tools, and states are not illegal or moral by themselves. Acts are what law can actually touch.

Examples: heroin is not illegal, possession may be. Cannabis is not illegal, acts around it may be. Cannibalism has no named UK offence, surrounding acts may be offences. AI is not harm, acts and uses are.

This is a legal-semantics paper. Very strong, very teachable.

3. The Shaded Classroom Problem

We did The Unused Tree, but the wider object is bigger:

How modern institutions fail to activate small nearby resources because the risk parser eats the affordance before the actual design is described.

This is not only drug-war. It could include heatwaves, floods, rooms, halls, gardens, workshops, spare batteries, local kitchens, old people with time, disabled people with place-knowledge.

Object: latent civic capacity.

4. The Good Bad Reader

Object: not hostile readers exactly, but readers whose predictable bad parsing must be designed around.

This comes up everywhere: man offering shade gets read wrong; psychosis pride gets read as pro-danger; cannabis benefit gets read as medical advice; God-union gets read as rank claim; autism language gets policed.

This could become a method paper:

Bad-Reader Risk as a Design Constraint

Very useful for your whole publication stack.

5. Language Police as Access Barrier

Object: language policing can exclude the people it claims to protect.

This is distinct from the sullied-metaphor paper. It is about rough speech, self-owned jokes, autistic directness, mad speech, reclaimed terms, and how "approved language" can become class/status filtering.

Core line: polishing the doorway can block the people the doorway was built for.

6. Evidence Is Not Person

Object: transcript integrity, file fragments, case notes, screenshots, prompts, and quotes are evidence objects, not whole-person proxies.

This overlaps object custody, but could stand alone as a public-facing essay. It fits legal, AI, psychosis, and internet conflict.

Core line: a fragment is not the timeline.

7. The Helper Who Must Not Appear

Object: some people are structurally useful but socially unusable.

Medical cannabis users, mad people, autistic people, disabled people, old people, odd men, ex-patients, rough speakers, non-credentialed experts. They may have time, land, skill, or local knowledge, but the social parser marks them awkward before their offer is heard.

This is near The Unused Tree, but wider and sharper.

8. The Anti-Charisma Paper

Object: good language, charm, fluency, credentials, calm tone, and social approval do not indicate good intent.

This is one of your best general-purpose social theory objects. It links cults, fascists, therapists, managers, priests, polished AI, and "respectable" abusers.

Core line: beautiful language is not evidence of good custody.

9. The Private Baseline

Object: Naked GPT, stock bots, baseline models, and A/B testing as epistemic tools.

This is not exactly object security. It is about why an unmodified baseline matters. You used Naked GPT not as a joke skin, but as a control condition. That is a real methods paper.

Possible title: Why Naked Baselines Matter

10. Small Gods, Small Tools

Object: humans use tiny symbolic machines to hold enormous world-pressure.

MOGRI, DRAGI, "Feed Baby", "trip abaters", "unused tree", "same psychosis, different azimuth", "weather". This is not the technical stack. It is about why small phrases can carry giant systems.

Possible title: Tiny Names for Giant Machines
